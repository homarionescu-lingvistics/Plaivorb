## 7. Material metodologic în limba engleză

> **Document-sursă integrat integral:** `The Dialectic of Dogma and Data: Computational Phonotactics, Structural Typology, and Political Ideology in European Historical Linguistics`
> Conținutul de mai jos este inclus ca material documentar; eventualele afirmații istorice, etimologice sau geopolitice rămân supuse verificării critice.


The history of European historical linguistics has often been shaped by a struggle between
empirical scientific analysis and political statecraft. Throughout the nineteenth and twentieth
centuries, the development of comparative-historical linguistics was closely aligned with the
rise of the nation-state, where linguistic narratives were frequently co-opted to validate
territorial claims, construct national identities, and establish civilizational hierarchies<sup>1</sup>.
Philologists and state actors alike routinely utilized genetic "tree models" of language descent
to imply cultural and biological purity, presenting languages as unhybridized lineages stretching
back to idealized antiquities, such as imperial Rome or a mythical Indo-European homeland<sup>2</sup>.
However, modern research across historical sociolinguistics, paleogenomics, and
computational typology demonstrates that language evolution is a process of deep
hybridization<sup>4</sup>. This analysis explores this tension by examining two primary case studies: the
Latinity and Balkan hybridization of Romanian, and the Indo-Europeanization and
pre-Indo-European substrate of German.
By applying a computational model that contrasts superficial phonetic proximity with deep
morphosyntactic typological distance, this report explores how the visible, high-prestige lexical
layer of a language (the adstrat) is frequently determined by political elites, whereas its
underlying grammatical "operating system" (the substrat) is driven by indigenous populations
and long-term regional contact<sup>4</sup>.
Theoretical Framework: The Prestige-Structure
Asymmetry
To analyze the relationship between political ideology and linguistic science, one must evaluate
the structural divergence between lexical vocabulary and grammatical architecture. Traditional
nationalistic philology has historically focused on the highly visible lexical adstrat—the
vocabulary of administration, religion, and high culture—to argue for "pure" genetic lineages<sup>1</sup>.
This bias is rooted in what may be termed the psychology of "prestige vassalage" (vasalitate de
prestigiu), wherein local intellectual elites align their language with a dominant global civilization
(such as Rome or the "Aryan" West) to escape a perceived "fear of isolation" (frica de izolare)<sup>4</sup>.
This focus, however, frequently ignores the deeper structural mechanics of language, which
are less susceptible to rapid elite manipulation<sup>4</sup>. When a population undergoes a language shift,
they typically adopt the prestigious vocabulary of the incoming elite but apply it using the
phonological constraints, simplified verbal paradigms, and syntactic templates of their nativetongue<sup>4</sup>.
This process of creolization or pidginization means that while a language’s lexicon may point
toward a prestigious imperial source, its functional syntax remains anchored to an indigenous
substrate or an areal linguistic zone, such as a Sprachbund<sup>4</sup>.
Chapter 1: The Romanian Case Study – Latinity, Balkan
Convergence, and Historiographical Divergence
The historical development of Romanian linguistics provides a clear example of how
grammatical and lexical data can be interpreted to serve competing political agendas.
Positioned at the intersection of the Romance, Slavic, and Balkan worlds, the Romanian
language has historically been analyzed through contrasting lenses: as a pure monument of
Roman imperial expansion, a late medieval immigrant tongue originating south of the Danube,
or a highly hybridized Balkan-Romance creation<sup>1</sup>.
The Geopolitics of Latinity: The Transylvanian School and Western
Alignment
During the eighteenth century, the Romanian-speaking majority in Transylvania faced
systematic political, economic, and religious marginalization under the dominant Hungarian
nobility, Saxon patricians, and Szeklers<sup>1</sup>. This dynamic began to shift after 1698, when the
Orthodox metropolitan Atanasie Anghel of Transylvania, along with 38 protopopes, aligned
themselves with Rome, establishing the Romanian Church United with Rome (Greco-Catholic)<sup>1</sup>.
This institutional connection gave Romanian scholars access to Catholic universities in Vienna
and Rome, bringing them into direct contact with the ideas of the European Enlightenment<sup>1</sup>.
This intellectual environment catalyzed the emergence of the Transylvanian School (Școala
Ardeleană), led by Greek-Catholic clerical intellectuals including Inocențiu Micu-Klein,
Gherontie Cotore, Grigorie Maior, Petru Pavel Aaron, Samuil Micu, and Petru Maior<sup>1</sup>. To
advocate for the political emancipation of Transylvanian Romanians (culminating in the petition
Supplex Libellus Valachorum), these scholars turned to historical philology<sup>1</sup>. They sought to
document the direct descent of the Romanians from Trajan’s Roman colonists, using this
historical priority to demand equal rights within the Transylvanian Diet<sup>1</sup>.
To highlight this Latin heritage, the Transylvanian School implemented systematic linguistic
reforms1:
● The Etymological Alphabet: They designed a Latin-alphabet orthography, first used in
Inocențiu Micu-Klein’s Book of Prayers, to replace the traditional Cyrillic script and earlier
Hungarian-based spelling rules<sup>1</sup>. This system was highly etymological, spelling words to
emphasize their classical Latin origins rather than their spoken phonetic forms<sup>16</sup>.
● Systematic Lexical Purism: Scholars initiated an extensive "purification" of the language,
attempting to purge non-Latin elements (particularly Slavic, Hungarian, and Turkish
loanwords) and replace them with classical Latin and Western Romance borrowings<sup>1</sup>.
This political mobilization of philology drew upon a longer tradition of Western observation.
Since the Middle Ages, Italian humanists, diplomats, and travelers had noted the linguisticsimilarities between Latin, Italian, and Romanian<sup>17</sup>. As early as the fifteenth century, during
efforts to organize Christian crusades against Ottoman expansion into Wallachia and Moldavia,
intellectuals like Flavio Biondo and Enea Silvio Piccolomini (later Pope Pius II) observed that the
Romanian language, though evolved, remained recognizable as a Romance tongue<sup>17</sup>.
In 1532, Francesco della Valle noted that the inhabitants of Transylvania, Moldavia, and Wallachia
referred to themselves as "Romei" and claimed descent from Rome<sup>17</sup>. This Latin identity was
also recorded by early Romanian chroniclers; in his Letopisețul Ţării Moldovei, Grigore Ureche
noted that Romanian was a neo-Latin language, citing basic lexical cognates such as pâine
(from Latin panis) and carne (from Latin caro)<sup>18</sup>.
The Purist Excesses and the Ideological Backlash
The extreme Latinist approach of the Transylvanian School and its nineteenth-century
successors eventually provoked significant intellectual resistance<sup>1</sup>. Scholars like Mihail
Kogălniceanu and Bogdan Petriceicu Hasdeu criticized the artificial purification of the
language<sup>1</sup>. Hasdeu ridiculed these excesses in his play Orthonerozia sau Trei crai de la răsărit,
lampooning the character Numa Consule, who spoke a highly artificial, Latinized jargon that
was unintelligible to ordinary Romanian speakers<sup>16</sup>.
This critique was supported by Ion Heliade Rădulescu, who, in the preface to his Grammar,
famously declared that "we write for the living, not for the dead," advocating for a standardized
literary language based on natural usage rather than archaic Latin reconstructions<sup>16</sup>.
Following the post-World War II Soviet occupation and the establishment of the Communist
regime, the politics of Romanian linguistics shifted again<sup>1</sup>. The regime banned the
Greek-Catholic Church, arrestingly labeling its former scholars as "non-Romanian agents of the
West" or "Hungarians"1.
To align Romanian historiography with Soviet-bloc pan-Slavism, communist ideologists
de-emphasized the Transylvanian School’s Latinist narrative<sup>1</sup>. They elevated a narrative of
"nationalist Orthodox resilience," asserting that the Romanian population survived foreign rule
through Orthodox Christian structures, while highlighting Slavic linguistic elements at the
expense of its Western Romance heritage<sup>1</sup>.
The Roeslerian Challenge and the Continuity Conflict
In the late nineteenth century, as regional nationalisms intensified, Austro-Hungarian and
Hungarian scholars sought to contest Romanian territorial claims in Transylvania<sup>3</sup>. In 1871, the
Austrian scholar Robert Roesler published Romänische Studien, formulating a comprehensive
"Immigrationist Theory"12. Roesler's thesis rested on several key claims13:
1. The indigenous Dacian population was entirely exterminated during the Roman conquest.
2. The Roman administration and civil population completely evacuated Dacia during the
Aurelian Retreat in 271 AD, leaving the territory north of the Danube devoid of Romance
speakers<sup>19</sup>.
3. The Romanian language and ethnic identity developed south of the Danube, in close
geographic contact with the ancestors of the Albanians, which explained the shared
Balkan vocabulary.4. Romanians only migrated north of the Danube as nomadic pastoralists starting in the
thirteenth century, arriving after Hungarian and Saxon settlements had been
established<sup>14</sup>.
To support this model, Roesler noted that no classical Roman toponyms (place-names)
survived in Transylvanian folk usage, pointing out that major settlements bore Hungarian,
Saxon, or Slavic names<sup>14</sup>. Pál Hunfalvy, a founder of comparative linguistics in Hungary,
popularized and expanded Roesler’s views to argue against Romanian historical priority in
Transylvania<sup>14</sup>.
Romanian scholars, from Alexandru D. Xenopol onward, countered this thesis by defending the
Continuity Theory<sup>12</sup>. They argued that the Romanization of Dacia occurred over a longer period,
that the Aurelian Retreat only involved the military and administrative elites, and that the shared
Romanian-Albanian linguistic elements reflected a common Paleo-Balkan substrate
(Dacian/Thracian) rather than a shared medieval homeland south of the Danube<sup>13</sup>.

rely on chronological stratigraphy<sup>19</sup>. By examining the earliest attestations of sound changes
and borrowing horizons, scholars can map the development of the language independently of
national myths<sup>19</sup>.

The Computational Verdict – Deconstructing "Purity"
Through Quantitative Evidence
To evaluate the tension between lexical adstrats and grammatical substrates, a quantitative
experiment was executed<sup>4</sup>. The computational model uses weighted phonetic edit distances
(based on customized ASJP sound classes) to analyze lexical proximity, and a permutation test
to detect potential analytical bias (p-hacking) in selected historical datasets<sup>4</sup>.
Additionally, the model calculates the normalized Manhattan distance across 12
morphosyntactic parameters to measure structural divergence between Romanian, Latin,
French, Albanian, and Bulgarian<sup>4</sup>.

# Metodologie computațională — distanțe fonetice / sintactice
import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial.distance import cityblock

# 1. MATRICEA DE CLASE FONETICE ȘI COSTURI DE SUBSTITUȚIE (Stil ASJP)

SOUND_CLASSES = {
'p': 'p', 'b': 'p', 'f': 'p', 'v': 'p',
# Labiale / Labiodentale
't': 't', 'd': 't', 's': 't', 'z': 't',
# Dentale / Alveolare
'k': 'k', 'g': 'k', 'q': 'k', 'x': 'k',
# Velare / Labiovelare
'm': 'n', 'n': 'n',
#### Nazale
'r': 'l', 'l': 'l',
#### Lichide
'a': 'v', 'e': 'v', 'i': 'v', 'o': 'v', 'u': 'v', 'ă': 'v', 'î': 'v' # Vocale
}


def get_phonetic_cost(char1, char2):
"""Calculează costul de substituție între două caractere."""
if char1 == char2:
return 0.0
class1 = SOUND_CLASSES.get(char1, char1)
class2 = SOUND_CLASSES.get(char2, char2)

if class1 == class2:
return 0.4 # Penalizare mică pentru aceeași clasă
# Cazul special: tranziția de la velară/labiovelară (k/q) la labială (p)

if (class1 == 'k' and class2 == 'p') or (class1 == 'p' and class2 == 'k'):
return 0.5 # Cost redus pentru a simula mutația PIE *kʷ -> p
return 1.0 # Schimbare fonetică radicală


# 2. DISTANȚA LEVENSHTEIN PONDERATĂ FONETIC

def weighted_levenshtein(s1, s2):
"""Calculează distanța de editare pe baza costurilor fonetice."""
m, n = len(s1), len(s2)
dp = np.zeros((m + 1, n + 1))


for i in range(m + 1): dp[i, 0] = i * 0.8 # Cost inserție/ștergere
for j in range(n + 1): dp[0, j] = j * 0.8

for i in range(1, m + 1):
for j in range(1, n + 1):
cost = get_phonetic_cost(s1[i-1], s2[j-1])
dp[i, j] = min(
dp[i-1, j] + 0.8,
#### Ștergere
dp[i, j-1] + 0.8,
#### Inserție
dp[i-1, j-1] + cost # Substituție
)

return dp[m, n]

def normalized_proximity(s1, s2):
"""Transformă distanța în proximitate (0 = diferit, 1 = identic)."""
max_len = max(len(s1), len(s2))

if max_len == 0: return 1.0
dist = weighted_levenshtein(s1, s2)

return max(0.0, 1.0 - (dist / (max_len * 0.8)))


# 3. TEST DE PERMUTARE (Pentru a dovedi coincidența / P-Hackingul)

def permutation_test(romanian_words, source_words, num_permutations=1000):
"""Verifică dacă alinierea dintre limba țintă și cea sursă este reală
sau dacă regulile fonetice sunt atât de largi încât s-ar potrivi
pe orice set de cuvinte amestecate aleatoriu.
"""
observed_proxed = []
for ro, src in zip(romanian_words, source_words):
observed_proxed.append(normalized_proximity(ro, src))
observed_mean = np.mean(observed_proxed)

null_distributions = []
words_copy = list(source_words)
np.random.seed(42) # Set seed for exact replication

for_in range(num_permutations):
np.random.shuffle(words_copy)
perm_proxed = [normalized_proximity(ro, src)
 for ro, src in zip(romanian_words,
words_copy)]
null_distributions.append(np.mean(perm_proxed))

p_value = np.sum(np.array(null_distributions) >= observed_mean) / num_permutations
return observed_mean, p_value

The development of Germanic historical linguistics during the nineteenth century was similarly
influenced by nationalist ideologies and the search for ancestral purity<sup>2</sup>.
The Ideological Mechanics of "Indo-Germanism"
Following the discovery of the Indo-European language family, nineteenth-century German
scholars coined the term Indogermanisch (Indo-Germanic), positioning the Germanic
languages at the center of this newly discovered family<sup>2</sup>.
This framework was quickly integrated into romantic nationalist myths2:
● The Aryan Migration Myth: Scholars constructed a narrative of an "Aryan" warrior race
that migrated from the Pontic steppes, conquering Neolithic Europe and replacing its
indigenous populations<sup>2</sup>.
● The Purity Narrative: German philologists argued that the Germanic tribes had
preserved the purest linguistic and cultural traditions of these Proto-Indo-European
ancestors<sup>2</sup>.
● Semantic Over-Fitting: To maintain this narrative of genetic purity, classical
etymologists frequently resorted to forced semantic etymologies<sup>4</sup>. Non-Indo-European
words were artificially traced to hypothetical Indo-European roots<sup>4</sup>. For example, the
common Germanic word for sea (English sea, German See, from Proto-Germanic
) was derived from the hypothetical PIE roots
("to drip") or
("pain" or "violence," to suggest a stormy sea), ignoring the ecological and maritime
realities of the Baltic region<sup>4</sup>.
Archaeological and Paleogenetic Reality: The Hybridization of the
North
Modern archaeology and paleogenomics have challenged this narrative of pure steppe
replacement, demonstrating that the Proto-Germanic speech community emerged from a
complex cultural and genetic fusion<sup>4</sup>.
Before the arrival of Indo-European speakers, Northern Europe was inhabited by
well-established Neolithic societies:
1. The Funnelbeaker Culture (Funnelbeaker / TRB, 4300–2800 BC): The first
agriculturalists of the North, characterized by megalithic architecture and highly
developed farming practices<sup>4</sup>.
2. The Pitted Ware Culture (Pitted Ware / PWC, 3200–2300 BC): Specialized coastal
hunter-gatherers and deep-sea fishers who occupied the Scandinavian coastlines<sup>4</sup>.
Around 2800 BC, pastoralist groups associated with the Corded Ware / Battle Axe culture
migrated into Northern Germany and Southern Scandinavia, bringing Indo-European dialectsand genetic haplogroups R1a and R1b<sup>4</sup>. Paleogenetic studies of ancient DNA indicate that these
steppe groups did not wipe out the indigenous population<sup>4</sup>. Instead, they merged over several
centuries<sup>4</sup>.
The most prominent genetic marker of this fusion is Haplogroup I1, a paternal lineage
indigenous to Mesolithic and Neolithic Europe that was entirely absent in the incoming steppe
pastoralists<sup>4</sup>. Today, this marker is carried by 35% to 40% of Scandinavian and German men,
providing genetic evidence of the integration of pre-Indo-European populations into the
Proto-Germanic speech community<sup>4</sup>.
Structural and Phonotactic Properties of the Pre-Germanic Substrate
This cultural and biological synthesis left a major imprint on the Proto-Germanic language,

Phonesthetic Blending: An Alternative to Substrate Borrowing
An alternative perspective in modern scholarship suggests that some historically obscure
Germanic terms may have arisen through internal cognitive and phonological processes rather
than direct external borrowing<sup>25</sup>.
For example, basic Germanic words like blood (Proto-Germanic
"something that
flows") and bone (Proto-Germanic
) lack clear cognates in other Indo-European
branches, leading some scholars to suggest a non-Indo-European origin<sup>25</sup>.
However, more recent analyses propose the mechanism of phonesthetic blending25:
● The /b-/ Phonestheme: In Germanic, a high concentration of body-related words begin
with the bilabial stop /b/ (e.g., beard, brain, breast, bosom, back, body), forming a
semantic-phonetic cluster known as a "word constellation"25.
● Lexicogenetic Blending: Words like blood and bone may have been formed by blending
this body-associated initial /b-/ phonestheme with pre-existing, clearly Indo-European
lexemes, such as Proto-Germanic
("something that flows") and
("stone")<sup>25</sup>.
This cognitive model indicates that some lexical anomalies may result from internal languageplay and phonetic associations rather than substrate borrowing, highlighting the complexity of
early Germanic lexicogenesis<sup>25</sup>.
Chapter 3: Conclusions: The Sociolinguistic Reality of
Hybridization
The comparative analysis of Romanian and German demonstrates that standard national
languages are often constructed political projects<sup>4</sup>. To establish international prestige and
domestic legitimacy, nineteenth-century nationalist movements systematically cultivated
"myths of origin," prioritizing the highly visible, prestigious lexical adstrat to claim linear descent
from idealized ancestral civilizations<sup>2</sup>.
This ideological focus on prestige vocabulary is driven by two key psychological factors:
1. Prestige Vassalage (Vasalitate de Prestigiu): The desire of local elites to align their
culture with a grand historical civilization (such as Rome or the "Aryan" West) to claim
civilizational authority<sup>4</sup>.
2. The Fear of Isolation (Frica de Izolare): The concern that acknowledging local, unique, or
hybridized structural substrates will isolate the nation from global geopolitical blocks<sup>4</sup>.
These political narratives, however, are contradicted by empirical linguistic and genetic data<sup>4</sup>.
As shown by the computational model, while the lexical adstrat of a language can be
influenced by political elites, its deeper syntactic and typological framework remains anchored
to its geographical and demographic realities<sup>4</sup>.
Whether through the restructuring of Latin grammar within the Balkan Sprachbund or the
simplification of Indo-European verbal morphology by pre-Indo-European agriculturalists in
Northern Europe, the deep structural operating system of a language is shaped by its speaking
populations<sup>4</sup>.
Ultimately, both Romanian and German are highly hybridized linguistic systems<sup>4</sup>. The "purity" of
European languages is an ideological construct<sup>2</sup>.
By integrating computational phonotactics, structural typology, archaeology, and
paleogenomics, modern historical linguistics can look past these nationalist myths, recognizing
language development as a process of continuous cultural and biological synthesis<sup>4</sup>.
